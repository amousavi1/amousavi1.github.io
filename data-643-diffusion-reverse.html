12.2 The Reverse Process

DATA 443/643 · Week 12 · PDF · Slides

MSE on the noise you added. T reverse steps. Lab 12 uses the oracle mean.

These notes match the lecture slides. Use (slides) on the course hub for the deck.

Generation walks the forward process backward. You start from xT~𝒩(0,𝐈) and, step by step, remove a bit of noise until you have x0.

1. Predict the noise, not the image

Ho et al. (DDPM) train a net εθ(xt,t) to guess the ε you added in note 12.1. The usual loss is

𝔼t,x0,ε\|ε−εθ(xt,t)\|2.

Predicting ε is equivalent (up to scaling) to predicting x0 or the score ∇xtlogq(xt). Pick one parameterization and stay consistent.

Training is ordinary regression. Sample t uniformly, form xt with the one-liner from 12.1, predict ε, take MSE. No adversary.

2. One reverse step

Given xt and a noise prediction, a typical mean for xt−1 is

xt−1=1αt(xt−1−αt1−α¯tεθ(xt,t))+σtz,

with z~𝒩(0,𝐈) for t>1, and σt from the schedule (often σt2=βt).

If you knew x0, the true posterior q(xt−1∣xt,x0) is Gaussian with a closed-form mean. Lab 12 uses that oracle mean for a single step so you can see denoising without training a U-Net.

The oracle is a teaching crutch. A trained εθ is what replaces the known ε (and therefore the known x0) at sample time.

3. Sampling cost

You pay T network calls (or fewer with DDIM / distilled samplers). That is the trade: stable regression training, slow ancestral sampling. GANs (Week 11) sample in one forward pass and fight mode collapse instead.

For a project, report the sampler (T, DDPM vs DDIM) next to the metric. Changing T at test time is part of the method.

4. Teaching this note

About 35 minutes at the board, then ~6 minutes of video.

  • 0–10 min. MSE on ε. Same xt formula as 12.1.
  • 10–22 min. Write one reverse-step mean. Circle the εθ term.
  • 22–33 min. Worked scalar reverse step, then the Lab 12 oracle story.
  • Then play Umar Jamil 13:00–16:36 (training loop, then sampling loop). Pause when he draws one denoising step.

5. Worked example

Reuse x0=1.0, α¯t=0.64, ε=2.0, so xt=2.0 as in note 12.1. Take αt=0.64 for a single-step cartoon (so αt=0.8, 1−αt=0.36, 1−α¯t=0.6).

Suppose εθ=ε=2.0 exactly, and drop σtz for the mean:

xt−1=10.8(2.0−0.360.6·2.0)=1.25(2.0−1.2)=1.25×0.8=1.0.

The reverse mean lands on x0. That is the oracle: if you predict the true noise, one algebraic step peels it off (here αt=α¯t, a first-step special case). At a generic t you do not jump all the way to x0; you move closer. Lab 12 checks ‖xt−x0‖ vs ‖x^−x0‖ at t=20 and wants the second smaller.

If εθ=0 (the net refuses to denoise), the subtracted term vanishes and you mostly rescale xt, which is not a sample from the data.

6. Where students get stuck

  • Training a U-Net in Lab 12. The lab uses the true posterior mean because you know x0.
  • Omitting t as an input to εθ. The same xt is a different problem at t=10 and at t=900.
  • Forgetting σtz on the way down and then wondering why every sample looks identical.

7. Video

Watch Umar Jamil, How diffusion models work, 13:00–16:36.

Pause on the training objective (predict the noise) and on the sampling loop (many reverse steps). Same URL as note 12.1; do not replay 7:35–13:00 unless needed.

8. Practice

  1. If εθ=ε exactly, what should happen to ‖xt−1−x0‖ relative to ‖xt−x0‖, in expectation, for mid-range t?

  2. Why add σtz on the way down instead of always taking the mean?

  3. You may not train a score network in Lab 12. What replaces εθ for that one reverse step?

  4. xt=3, αt=0.25, α¯t=0.25, εθ=2, no extra noise. Compute the reverse mean. (αt=0.5, 1−α¯t=0.75≈0.866, 1−αt=0.75.)

  5. Training samples t uniformly from 1…T and one ε. Why is that enough, instead of a full reverse chain inside the loss?
