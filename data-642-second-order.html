2.3 Second-Order Methods

DATA 442/642 · Week 2 · PDF

Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, and BFGS.

1. Newton

Approximate J by a quadratic at the current point θ(k):

J(θ(k)+δ)≈J(θ(k))+δ⊤∇J(θ(k))+12δ⊤H(θ(k))δ,

where H=∇2J. Minimize that quadratic: solve

H(θ(k))δ=−∇J(θ(k)),

then θ(k+1)=θ(k)+δ. On a convex quadratic, one Newton step is exact. That is the point of Lab 2, exercise 3: a badly scaled bowl that GD crawls and Newton finishes.

2. When Newton breaks

If H is not positive definite, the quadratic is not a bowl and the step can go uphill or blow up. A cheap fix is to solve (H+λI)δ=−∇J (Levenberg–Marquardt). Large λ looks like gradient descent. Small λ looks like Newton.

Analytical second derivatives are often unpleasant. Finite differences are possible and do not guarantee an SPD H.

3. Quasi-Newton and BFGS

Do not form H. Keep a symmetric positive-definite stand-in H* for H−1, update it from the observed Δθ and Δ(∇J), and set

δ(k)=−H*(θ(k))∇J(θ(k)),

then line-search. BFGS is the update you should be able to name. Rank-two, stays SPD, no explicit Hessian. Faster and more stable than raw Newton on many medium-scale problems; still heavier than SGD on a million-example net.

Module 6 will come back to first-order methods (Adam, RMSProp). The idea is the same: better than raw −∇J, cheaper than H−1.
